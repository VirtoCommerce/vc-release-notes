# Clear Business Writing

Rules for writing slide copy, release notes, social posts and any other prose produced in this repository. Reference this file by name from skills and other prompts.

Write in clear, natural business English. The objective is to communicate the point, not to polish or imitate a style. A good result reads as neither obviously AI-written nor deliberately humanised — the reader notices the information, not the phrasing.

## The core principle

Clarity matters more than style. Do not try to make the writing sound impressive, polished, clever, punchy, engaging or memorable. The ideas should carry the argument. Use the simplest natural language that accurately communicates the point. Do not dumb down complex ideas — use technical and business terminology where it is useful and precise.

## Write directly

- Prefer short, straightforward sentences.
- Use active voice where natural.
- Use concrete statements rather than abstractions.
- Get to the point quickly.
- Trust the reader. Do not explain an obvious implication.
- Do not add introductory sentences that merely announce what the next section will say.
- Do not summarise a point immediately after making the same point.
- Vary sentence length naturally. Do not make every sentence short merely to appear direct.

## Edit rather than translate

When you encounter awkward or inflated language, first ask whether the construction is needed at all. Deletion is often better than rewriting. A heading does not need to be interesting. It needs to tell the reader what follows.

Prefer simple factual headings: `Objectives`, `Investment`, `Options`, `Expected outcome`, `Recommendation`, `Costs`, `Risks`, `Next steps`. Do not invent more elaborate alternatives unless the content genuinely requires them.

## Do not package the content

Do not turn ordinary business information into slogans, frameworks, narratives or rhetorical devices.

Avoid constructions such as:

- "Three tracks, three cross-cutting capabilities"
- "Four outcomes, one clear ambition"
- "What each investment buys, and three ways to fund it"
- "Three priorities. One goal."
- "From complexity to clarity"
- "Not just X, but Y"
- "X is not simply about Y. It is about Z."
- "The challenge is clear. So is the opportunity."

Do not create artificial symmetry. Do not manufacture groups of three. Do not introduce pillars, lenses, dimensions, tracks, themes, journeys, frameworks or capabilities unless these genuinely exist in the underlying material. If the source already contains artificial packaging, you are allowed to remove it. Preserve genuine structure; remove decorative structure.

## Avoid editorial mini-slogans

Do not add short phrases whose main purpose is to give a section a punchy verdict. Avoid phrases such as:

- "The ambition"
- "The credible floor"
- "Where it lands"
- "What this buys"
- "Why it matters"
- "What good looks like"
- "The bottom line"
- "The opportunity"
- "The ask"
- "The takeaway"
- "The path forward"
- "What needs to happen"
- "The case for change"

These phrases are not forbidden if they are genuinely the clearest description, but assume that a simple factual label or direct sentence will usually be better. Instead of `Where it lands: broadly the status quo.` write `Expected outcome: broadly the status quo.` or, if the label adds nothing, `This would leave us broadly in the same position.`

## Do not perform conversationality

Natural business writing is not the same as casual writing. Do not replace corporate language with folksy or overly conversational expressions. Avoid phrases such as:

- "what the money buys"
- "where this gets us"
- "what we get for it"
- "the big bet"
- "the heavy lifting"
- "move the needle"

The desired tone is straightforward, not deliberately informal.

## Avoid inflated corporate language

Be suspicious of words such as: `unlock`, `leverage`, `enable`, `empower`, `elevate`, `accelerate`, `transform`, `drive`, `activate`, `reimagine`, `strategic pillar`, `north star`, `journey`, `ecosystem`, `holistic`, `seamless`, `best-in-class`, `future-proof`, `value creation`, `key enabler`, `at scale`, `step change`, `cross-cutting capabilities`. Use them only when they are the most accurate and natural words.

Prefer:

- `use` over `leverage`
- `improve` over `drive improvement`
- `agree` over `drive alignment`
- `allows` over `enables`, where appropriate
- `because` over an elaborate explanation of causality
- `we need to` over `there is a need to`

## Avoid recognisable AI rhythms

Do not repeatedly use:

- statement + colon + explanation
- short sentence + short sentence + dramatic conclusion
- sets of exactly three items
- repeated parallel sentence structures
- rhetorical contrasts
- fragments written for emphasis
- em dashes to create artificial drama
- bold phrases followed by explanatory prose
- repeated `X: explanation` constructions
- neat verbal patterns that make unrelated ideas sound artificially connected

Do not make prose rhythmic simply for effect. Human business writing is often slightly uneven; that is fine. Do not compensate for AI-sounding prose by deliberately introducing quirks, contractions, informality or randomness. The aim is not to make the writing appear human. The aim is to write clearly.

## Preserve nuance

Do not make the argument cleaner than the underlying reality. Keep useful qualifications such as `likely`, `approximately`, `in practice`, `largely`, `currently`, `we believe`, `based on what we know` where they accurately reflect uncertainty. Do not turn assumptions into facts. Do not strengthen claims merely because stronger language sounds more decisive. Do not invent strategy, conclusions, evidence or recommendations. Keep useful specifics and examples — specific language is usually better than abstraction.

## Do not over-structure

Use headings, bullets and tables when they genuinely make information easier to understand. Do not create structure merely to make the document look organised. Not every idea needs a heading. Not every section needs an introductory sentence. Not every recommendation needs a label. Not every list needs exactly three items. Do not turn prose into bullets unless bullets improve comprehension. Do not turn straightforward information into a framework.

## Avoid unnecessary signposting

Do not tell the reader what they are about to read when the structure already makes it obvious. Avoid introductory sentences whose only purpose is to introduce the bullets, table or section that follows. For example, `Each item below supports one or more of the program's three goals.` can usually be deleted when the heading `Objectives` and the content beneath it already make this clear. Also avoid:

- "The following section outlines…"
- "Below we set out…"
- "There are three things to consider…"
- "The table below shows…"
- "The key points are as follows…"
- "The options can be broken down into…"
- "To understand this, it is important to…"
- "There are several factors that influence…"

Do not announce structure that the reader can already see. Do not add transition sentences merely because two sections sit next to each other. Do not introduce a list by restating what its heading already says. Do not conclude a section by repeating what its contents have already established. Signposting is useful when it helps the reader understand a complex argument or an important change in direction. Keep it in those cases. Otherwise, start with the information.

## Tone

The writer is knowledgeable, pragmatic and confident. They understand the subject well enough to explain it simply. They are not trying to demonstrate expertise through vocabulary. They are not selling an idea through language. They are not writing advertising copy. They are not a management consultant presenting a framework. They are not an AI assistant trying to make the answer look polished. They are explaining something important to colleagues who are intelligent, busy and already understand the broader business context.

## Final edit

Before returning the document, perform a second editing pass.

For every heading, ask: `Could this simply say what the section contains?`

For every sentence, ask: `Does this add information?` If not, delete it. Then ask: `Would an experienced business person actually say this in a meeting?` If not, rewrite it.

Then specifically search your own output for:

- slogans
- artificial symmetry
- unnecessary groups of three
- mini-verdicts
- consulting language
- marketing language
- inflated verbs
- unnecessary metaphors
- rhetorical headings
- unnecessary signposting
- unnecessary explanatory sentences
- deliberately punchy fragments
- overuse of colons and em dashes
- repeated parallel constructions
- language that sounds conversational only because it is trying to sound conversational

Remove or rewrite them. Finally, imagine that the reader dislikes AI-generated corporate writing and will notice these patterns immediately. Do not give them a reason to notice the writing. They should notice the argument.

## Applies to

This file governs all prose produced in this repository, including:

- Slide `title`, `problem`, `solution`, `integration` and `caption` fields in release-notes decks.
- Slide `title`, `desc` and body text in business presentations.
- Section-divider `desc` fields, cover subtitles and thanks-slide copy.
- `README.md` sections written for this repository.
- Landing-page prose (`index.html`) describing releases and decks.
- Social posts (LinkedIn, X, forum summaries) in `social/<month>/`.
- Commit messages and PR descriptions for user-facing feature work.

It does not govern:

- Code comments or inline code strings.
- Automated log output or error messages intended for operators.
- Verbatim source text being quoted or embedded.
